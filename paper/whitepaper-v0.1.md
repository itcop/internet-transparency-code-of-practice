# White paper for ITCoP.org: Transparency by Default. The Internet Transparency Stack, co-regulated identification, and Operational Transparency for AI


By Mark Lizar and Michele Drgon
Transparency and Consent Interoperability Group (TCIEG)
Version 0.1 for external review, 31 August 2026

Author note. Mark Lizar is a member of the Canadian mirror committee to ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 27/WG 5 and a contributor to the Kantara Initiative Anchored Notice and Consent Receipt Work Group. This interest is declared. Michele Drgon is a co-founder of the Transparency and Consent Interoperability Group.

## Executive summary
The internet runs on a promise. Accept the policy, hand over the data, and trust that accountability exists somewhere behind the interface. That promise is now failing in public, because artificial intelligence banks human knowledge at a scale no privacy policy was written to govern, and because personal data crosses borders faster than any regulator can follow it.
The question a person should be able to answer before identifying themselves, is who is accountable for this, for what purpose, under what authority, and where does my personal information go. Currently there are no answers that anyone can inspect. 
This paper sets out the correction in three connected parts.
- The Internet Transparency Stack is a layered reference architecture that runs from law or treaty obligation down to machine readable record, and from record back up to regulator. It locates the missing layer precisely: the connection between a conformant consent record and the law or treaty obligation it is supposed to satisfy.
- Co-regulated identification is what that architecture makes possible at the point of interaction. Two rule sets operate on the same identifier, the organisation's own rules and the public rules of treaty, law and standards, with the public rules expressed as a record structure that can be inspected before identification is demanded.
- Operational Transparency for AI applies the same mechanism to systems deployed by many parties across many jurisdictions. Every participant gives notice for the scope of work it performs, and that notice becomes the input the next participant builds against and tests.
The common element is a record generated by the operation rather than a document written about it afterwards. Transparency that cannot be checked is merely a promise. Transparency that produces a record is evidence.
None of this requires a new protocol, a new identification technology, or the disclosure of trade secrets, model weights or dataset contents. It requires that the governance record about a data flow be as portable as the data flow itself.

## 1. Governance that arrives too late
Today, almost every digital interaction now begins with the same demand: Identify yourself, log in, scan a face, create an account, accept the terms. Only afterwards, if at all, does a person learn who is accountable, what is processed, on what authority, and where it travels.     
Traditional privacy frameworks assume a visible relationship. An individual knows the organisation, reads a notice, makes a choice, and then discloses personal data. Today, the user-facing service is increasingly only the visible edge of a distributed processing environment. Distributed systems and cloud native architectures no longer operate in that order. Identification, profiling, inference, automated processing and cross border disclosure begin before the individual has any means of establishing who is accountable or where their personal data is going.
This is usually treated as a compliance problem. It is better understood as a problem of timing. Rules that can only be checked after processing cannot govern processing. They can only characterise it afterwards, once the data has moved, once the model has been trained, and once the commercial position has been established. In machine learning, the timing is decisive, because once data has shaped a model, withdrawing it is no longer a simple act of deletion. A defect in the legal authority to use that data can therefore propagate into models and downstream deployments built from it.
The physical world solved this a long time ago and solved it the same way each time. The Forest Charter of 1217 did not invent new rules. It required the boundary of forest authority to be established so that people could know when they were entering a place governed by special rules, and whose authority applied there. Centuries later the receipt did something similar for transactions: it made the other party’s claim and obligation visible and gave the individual evidence to keep. Both corrections followed the same form. Make authority knowable at the threshold, make the claim inspectable at the point of contact, and give the weaker party evidence of it.
Online, these protections do not exist: no visible boundary before entry, and no receipt held by the individual after the interaction.

## 2. The Internet Transparency Stack
The stack is a layered reference architecture, presented to ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 27/WG 5 at the June 2026 plenary. Read it from the bottom up. Each layer makes the layer above it possible. Authority cascades down from treaty to record, and evidence informs upward from the receipt to the regulator.
  | Layer | Instrument | Function |
  | 7. Enforcement | Internet Transparency Code of Practice (ITCoP) | Treaty or legal obligations made operational for digital environment |
  | 6. Treaty | Convention 108+ and CETS 225 | Cross border transparency and AI governance obligations |
  | 5. Evidence | ANCR extension to ISO/IEC TS 27560 | Machine readable proof of disclosure, authority and rights context |
  | 4. Record | ISO/IEC TS 27560:2023 | Consent record and receipt information structure |
  | 3. Presentation | ISO/IEC 29184 | Online privacy notice and consent content and structure |
  | 2. Framework | ISO/IEC 29100 | Technology-neutral privacy framework with terminology, actors and roles |
  | 1. Foundation | OECD cross-border guidelines, Convention 108 | Notice and awareness principles, carried by every instrument since |

### Why a standard, and not only a law
Convention 108+ Article 14 governs transborder flows and permits them where an appropriate level of protection is secured, including by standardised safeguards that can be demonstrated in advance rather than assessed case by case after the fact. A safeguard that must be demonstrable before a flow proceeds has to resolve to something specified, and the specification is an international standard.
That is the hinge of the whole architecture. The binding force does not have to come from the standard. It comes from the treaty, and the standard is what the obligation resolves to. On a Council of Europe panel at CPDP 2026 in Brussels this reading was confirmed by the Vice Convenor of ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 27/WG 5. 
It also explains why the guidance status of the AI privacy standards is not the weakness it first appears to be. A guidance document that carries a normative reference binds any party claiming conformity with it, and fixes the artefact rather than leaving each implementer to invent one.
The reach of the treaty layer is worth stating precisely, because it is regularly overstated. Convention 108 has more than fifty five parties. The modernising protocol has been ratified by thirty four. CETS 225, the Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law, was opened for signature on 5 September 2024 and has a separate signature and ratification position, so it should not be described as binding in the same breath as Convention 108+.

## 3. Co-regulated identification
The stack describes the architecture. Co-regulated identification is what it does at the interaction point where a person meets a system.

### Identity is human-managed, identification is technical
The distinction is the foundation of everything that follows, and conflating it is the single most common error in current digital identity work.
- Identity is self-expression about how a person self-identifies who they are, what they represent, etc.  It is managed by the individual.
- Identification is the technical and organisational process a controller uses to distinguish or recognise a person by assigning, receiving, resolving, or associating identifiers and attributes with that person, their account, credential, device, or activity.
- Consent is the expression, declaration or indication of a decision or preference made by a human person. Permissions are system configurations that enforce expressed human consent in systems.
Digital consent becomes possible only when human-driven, freely expressed choices are kept separate from the technical controls and implementations used to identify, index, track and process individuals, and to enforce permissions.
When human consent and technical permissions are conflated, systems treat repeated permission requests as if they were meaningful expressions of consent. That is what produces cookie-banner consent fatigue. A technical permission cannot itself prove the human choice or authority it is supposed to implement. Keeping the two distinct in the record is what makes that relationship testable rather than merely asserted.


### Two rule sets on one identifier
Co-regulation  rebalances the privacy relationship between individuals and controllers. Rather than only controllers defining the terms under which they will process individuals’ personal data, now individuals have the ability to self-regulate  - under a different set of rules
- The controller's regulation through their own rules: privacy policy, terms of service terms, technical design, internal policy.
- The individual’s self-regulation using public rules: the Convention 108+ baseline, applicable law, and the standards profile that makes disclosure machine-readable and inspectable.
<paragraph>
Neither self-regulation nor law alone governs identification well at internet scale. Self-regulation still leaves identifiers and their use defined by the controller. Law can set the obligations, but does not by itself provide the technical records needed to verify them at the point of identification. Co-regulation combines the two: public requirements expressed in a common record structure, implemented by controllers and verifiable before identification occurs.
This is additive for the identity management industry, not restrictive. It specifies no identification technology, requires no new protocol, and leaves the existing credential and wallet ecosystem in place. The problem is not identification itself. It is identification without a visible and verifiable accountability layer around it.

### The inversion: controller identification first
The operating rule is one sentence. Verify who is accountable before any identification is demanded.
- The Controller Identification Record is disclosed and resolvable before any demand for personal identification.
- The individual, or an agent acting for the individual, can inspect who is accountable, what is claimed, what processing is processed, on what authority, and where recourse exists.
- Only then does the interaction proceed under its stated lawful basis: consent, contract, legal obligation, legitimate interest, vital interest or public interest, as applicable.

### The artefact set
  | Artefact | Function under co-regulation |
  | Controller Identification Record | Public accountability anchor, resolvable before identification is demanded |
  | Notice Record | Versioned, machine-readable transparency statement. The public rule’s transparency requirements, made operational and inspectable |
  | Anchored Notice Receipt | Bilateral evidence that a specific notice version was disclosed, anonymous by default |
  | Notice Event Log | Append-only lifecycle record: issuance and material change, with hooks for withdrawal and objection |
  | Authorization State Object | Current authorization state, evaluable at the point of execution rather than inferred from an earlier interaction |
Together these provide minimum notice disclosure: the smallest set of disclosures that must be present and inspectable before identification or processing begins.

### Proportionate disclosure as the measurable test
Co-regulation sets a measurable standard for the disclosure itself. What the technology can do must be matched by what the individual can see and control: the technology class in use, the practical controls actually available, associated or derivative controls, secondary purposes of use, and any limitation or derogation on those controls.
Where the capability of the technology exceeds the controls disclosed, the disclosure is not proportionate, and the identification is not defined and governed according to by all stakeholders. This is a test, not an aspiration, and it can be applied by a party that had no part in building the system.

### Online consent, and the case that is not online consent
Two cases are routinely confused, and the distinction decides how a record must be constructed.
Online notice in a digital identification context, principal initiated, where two factor notice returns a bilateral Anchored Notice Receipt. The individual approaches the sign, the access point, the device or the online notice in order to discover. The disclosure event defaults to online consent unless another basis is asserted, because an individual who seeks out and reads a public notice is doing so to discover, and notice has always worked this way. Absent a sign, approach and looking are ordinarily permitted, and it is the sign that changes the default. The digital case introduces no new claim. It makes the default recordable, provides operational transparency for dynamic active state authorization, and accommodates multiple legal bases.
Offline consent presented through an online interface, where identity and location are assumed rather than recorded. Here there is no default. The basis is asserted explicitly, a distinct authorization record is required, and the arrangement is governed by the applicable data protection regulation, captured in a controller record of processing activity that is private and not accessible by default.
Three consequences follow from the first case. The burden sits with the controller, so where another lawful basis applies the controller notifies at the point of disclosure by asserting that basis in the receipt header, and silence is not an assertion. The default is an interpretation of the notice event and not proof of legal consent for processing, so where a secondary purpose relies on consent that authorization is a separate record bound to the anchored one. And controller initiated disclosure is a different case entirely, where the operational default does not apply.

### Anonymity and inclusion
Two properties follow from the frame, and they are properties rather than exceptions granted by a controller.
Anonymity is the default state of the evidence. The Anchored Notice Receipt is generable and verifiable without an account identifier or a PII principal identifier, and retrieving a Controller Identification Record is not itself an identification event. Where a controller cannot produce evidence of notice without identifying the individual, the evidence has been made contingent on the very act it exists to govern.
Inclusion means notice and recourse do not depend on holding digital identification. The privacy access point is specified as a set of modalities rather than a single channel, so that the accountable controller, the notice version in effect, and the route to recourse stay reachable by people who cannot, or choose not to, hold a credential. This covers accessibility needs, low or absent connectivity, delegated and assisted interaction, and in person contexts. An implementation that offers recourse only through an authenticated digital channel excludes the people most exposed to the risk it exists to govern.

## 4. Operational Transparency for AI
Governance of digital systems currently operates diachronically. Legal authority is asserted at one time, technical operation occurs at another, and the individual's awareness, if it arrives, arrives at a third. Each element sits in a different moment, in a different document, held by a different party. Reconciliation is possible only by reconstruction, which is why regulators describe themselves as working like archaeologists.
Operational Transparency is the alternative. It is transparency expressed as records generated by the operation rather than documents written about it afterwards, and the property those records produce is synchronic. At the point of interaction, three things hold at once and are evidenced at once:
- the accountable party is identified and its authority is published in advance;
- the purpose, processing locations and lawful basis are disclosed in a form both a person and a machine can parse;
- the individual holds a record of what was presented and what was agreed, independent of the party that presented it.
A system that cannot present its terms before it acts cannot obtain consensus, whatever it later records.

### Notice through the chain of participants
The operating rule that separates this from disclosure as currently practised is that every participant in an AI system gives notice for the scope of work it performs. The designer discloses design scope and intended context. The developer discloses what it built and on what data authority. The party that trains discloses training scope and provenance. The deployer discloses purpose, decision scope and oversight. Each notice binds to the party that issued it and to the version in force when it was issued.
That notice is not filed. It is the input the next participant builds against and tests. A developer implements against the notice received from design and records whether the stated scope held. A deployer tests the notice received from development against what the system actually does in operation. Where the two diverge, the divergence is visible at the point it occurs and attributable to the participant that caused it, rather than surfacing years later as an unattributable defect in a finished system.
This changes what disclosure is for. Notice today is issued once, at the end of the chain, by whichever party faces the individual, describing a system that party did not build and cannot fully account for. Notice given per scope of work, and tested by the next participant, produces something the end disclosure can be derived from and checked against.

### The evidence gap, measured
The claim that AI governance is retrospective can be tested against the record.
Provenance is not merely unpublished, it is unreliable. The Data Provenance Initiative audited more than 1,800 widely used text datasets and reported licence omission rates above 70 percent and licence error rates above 50 percent. Of the incorrect licences, 29 percent were more permissive than the dataset creators intended. Where permission is recorded without a governance record capable of being checked, the recorded permission drifts in the permissive direction. Restrictive licensing was also found to concentrate in low resource languages and creative work, so the failure falls hardest on the least represented contributors.
Permission defects propagate into the model. EDPB Opinion 28/2024 addresses the anonymity of AI models, the conditions for relying on legitimate interest, and the effect of unlawful processing during development on the lawfulness of deployment. Provenance and permission are material to the deployed model, not only to the dataset. The absence of evidence at collection becomes a liability at operation.
Existing instruments require provenance without specifying an interoperable record.
  | Instrument | What it requires | Who holds the evidence |
  | EU AI Act Article 53(1)(d) | A publicly available, sufficiently detailed summary of training content on a Commission template | The provider, as narrative summary |
  | EU AI Act Article 53(1)(c), with Article 4(3) of Directive 2019/790 | A copyright policy identifying and honouring machine readable rights reservations | The provider, against no agreed record format |
  | EDPB Opinion 28/2024 | A documented three step legitimate interest assessment and impact assessment | The controller, internally |
  | ISO/IEC 42001:2023 | A documented process for recording data provenance and preparation | The organisation, verified by periodic audit |
In every case the evidence stays with the party that benefits from it, and in every case the record is a summary or an assessment rather than an artefact the affected person can produce.
Comparable records are demonstrably feasible. Content provenance credentials specified by the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity are in production across camera manufacturers, editing tools, platforms and model providers, and travel with content as cryptographically signed assertions. This establishes both that a portable provenance record can be standardised at scale, and the asymmetry that motivates this paper. Output provenance is being standardised. Permission provenance is not. Systems are being built to prove where a file came from while remaining unable to show whether the people described in it, or whose work produced it, were ever informed.

### The route through ISO/IEC 27091
ISO/IEC 27091 gives guidance on privacy protection for AI systems, covering the lawful basis for training data, information to PII principals, support for consent management, and retention that accounts for withdrawal. What it recommends is right. The record is left unspecified, so the evidence stays internal to whichever party produced it. The guidance is correct and the timing is wrong.
The ANCR extension to ISO/IEC TS 27560 supplies the missing record, and it can be embedded in 27091 rather than run alongside it: cited as a normative reference, with the record structure named in the guidance on AI models and in the privacy engineering clauses, and a worked case carried in the use case annex. On that basis 27091 becomes capable of synchronic governance instead of retrospective documentation, because each recommendation it makes acquires a record created when the processing occurs and held by more than one party.

### Regulated knowledge management
The most consequential case is the use of human knowledge as training material. Text, images, voice, questions, working practice and professional judgement are extracted and used to build systems then sold back into the same markets. Almost no one agreed to this in any sense a court or a citizen would recognise. It proceeded on permission language written earlier for a different purpose.
Regulated knowledge management has three requirements.
- Provenance transparency. The origin of training data is disclosed at a level that lets a person or a regulator establish whether their contribution is in scope.
- Permission transparency. The authority relied on for each purpose, including secondary purposes such as model training, is evidenced by a record created at the time of collection rather than asserted afterwards. Convention 108+ Article 5.4 provides the treaty basis for governing compatible secondary purpose.
- Standard transparency. The vocabulary and the record structure are the same across parties and jurisdictions, so the record is portable and the same question returns the same answer wherever it is asked.
The third is the facilitator, and it runs as a sequence in which each step depends on the one before. Standard transparency produces a machine readable record. A machine readable record is what makes identification transparent in both directions, since the party requesting identity can disclose and be verified before the credential is presented rather than after. Transparency in both directions is what makes governance interoperable between domains and jurisdictions, because a record written to a common structure can be evaluated by a party that was not present when it was created. Interoperability is what allows the model to scale beyond the organisations willing to submit to audit. And scale is where the security effect appears.
That security argument runs opposite to the usual one. Data protection places the burden on the individual and treats identification as the precondition of a relationship, so every service accumulates identifiers it must then defend, and the accumulated identifier becomes the only durable evidence that a relationship existed. Transparency first inverts the order. The accountable party discloses and is verifiable before collection, permission is evidenced on both sides, and oversight becomes a query against published records rather than an investigation reconstructing events after the fact.
This is the line between dirty AI and clean AI. Dirty AI trains on data with no consent and no record, accountability you are told exists. Clean AI carries a receipt, accountability you can verify. Markets cannot reward ethical AI until there is an inspectable signal to reward.

### The AI receipt
That claim has now been made twice, so the receipt should be shown. A receipt that cannot be displayed is the same kind of assertion this paper objects to everywhere else.
An AI receipt is not a new artefact and not a new protocol. It is the Anchored Notice Receipt of section 3, issued for a scope of work in an AI lifecycle rather than for a single disclosure to a single person. Each participant issues one for what it actually did, bound to the notice version in force at the time and to the authority relied on for the material it used. It is generated by the operation rather than written about it afterwards, it is held by more than one party, and it is verifiable without the party asking having to identify themselves.
Two forms exist, because two different things are used as training material and they are governed in different registers.
- A personal data receipt, where the training material contains personal data. The authority is a lawful basis, the record binds to a notice version and a purpose, and the individual holds a copy independent of the controller.
- A knowledge reuse receipt, where the training material is published work. The authority is a licence and a terms version, and the counterpart of the individual's copy is the producible record described in 8.2. Condition MRT-3 and the personal data case are the same requirement expressed in two registers, which is why the licence in section 8 is not a separate topic bolted onto this paper.
The minimum content is small enough to state in full.
  | Field | What it binds |
  | receipt_schema | The receipt structure and version, so a reader knows what to parse before parsing it |
  | participant_role | Designer, developer, trainer or deployer. Which link in the chain this receipt accounts for |
  | scope_of_work | What that participant did, in the terms it gave notice for |
  | controller_identification_record_id | The accountable party, resolvable without authentication |
  | notice_version_reference | The immutable version of the notice in force when the work was done, which is what makes a later claim checkable |
  | data_authority | Lawful basis for personal data, or licence and terms version for published work |
  | received_notice_references | The notices this participant built against, which is what turns a set of receipts into a chain |
  | recipient_jurisdictions, transfer_mechanism | Where the material goes and on what transfer authority |
  | authorization_state_id | Pointer to the Authorization State Object, so current validity is evaluable rather than frozen at issuance |
  | evidence_request_endpoint | Where the provenance record can be requested, without the requester identifying themselves |
  | integrity | Notice hash and signature, so the receipt and the notice it refers to cannot drift apart silently |
What the receipt buys is the ability to ask three questions of a deployed system and get answers that can be checked by someone who had no part in building it. On whose authority was this material used. Which version of which notice was in force at the time. And where do I go to see the record, without being identified in order to ask. A worked example along a four participant chain is set out in Annex A.
Note what the receipt does not contain. It carries no model weights, no dataset contents, no trade secrets and, in the anonymous case, no identifier for the person asking. The objection that provenance transparency requires disclosing proprietary material does not survive contact with the actual record structure.

## 5. What this Code of Practice asks of implementers
Nothing here requires a new protocol or a change of identification technology. It asks for four things to be published and kept current.
- Publish a Controller Identification Record. Resolvable without authentication, available before any demand for personal identification, carrying the accountable name, the jurisdiction, and at least one route to recourse that operates without a digital identification credential.
- Version the notice, and reference the version. Every disclosure event binds to an immutable notice version reference, and a material change creates a new version rather than editing the previous one in place.
- Issue a receipt that does not require identification. The evidence of disclosure is generable and verifiable without an account identifier or a PII principal identifier.
- Keep an event log. Issuance and material change at minimum, so a claim about a past interaction can be checked against the notice version actually in effect at the time.
An organisation that does these four things can demonstrate conformance per notice version, without exposing personal data in order to do it. An organisation that cannot do them is asking to be trusted rather than inspected.

### Three things a static disclosure cannot do
The Code of Practice addresses three conditions that a published policy document cannot meet on its own.
Dynamic transparency, because the disclosure state changes after the notice is issued. A model is updated, a purpose is extended, a recipient or processing location is added, a risk posture changes. The record has to remain evaluable rather than standing as a snapshot of a moment that has passed.
Consent signalling, because permission has to be expressed in a form a system reads and acts on before processing, rather than held as a preference on one side of the interaction.
Permissions for active state workflows, because agentic and continuously learning systems act between interactions. Authority must be resolvable at the point of execution, delegation must carry its scope, expiry and revocation route, and an operation whose authority cannot be resolved must fail closed.
The rule that governs all three states the operational transparency principle in one line: what is inspectable in person must be verifiable online, before identification is demanded and before any cross border transfer occurs.

### The point of entry is a document every organisation already has
Upgrading a privacy policy to this standard produces a digital privacy policy: the same commitments, expressed as the published controller record and the notice structures the Code specifies, versioned and resolvable, so that the policy and the operation refer to the same thing rather than to each other by description. The legal document does not go away and its drafting is not delegated to a schema. What changes is that the commitments become inspectable at the point of interaction.

### From self assertion to verification
A Controller Identification Record is published by the controller about itself, so on its own it remains a self assertion. What makes the claim testable is verification by a registry: proof of domain control and of legal entity, after which a jurisdiction scoped controller registry identifier is issued and every record that controller produces resolves to it.
That is the first assurance transition, from self assertion to registry verification, with cryptographic signature and active state validation above it. At the top level, current validity is evaluable in real time as valid, invalid or suspended, which is the level dynamic AI contexts require, since a receipt issued last month cannot represent a risk posture that changed last week.
The jurisdiction component of the identifier is what carries this across borders. Where the verified entity's jurisdiction is part of its identifier, a receipt issued under one authority can be evaluated against the authority claimed in another, which is the check a transfer disclosure currently asks a person to take on trust.
Co-regulation sits exactly at that transition. Public authority sets the rule, an accredited registry verifies who the accountable party is, and each participant generates the notice records for its own scope of work. None of the three substitutes for the other two, which is why neither regulation alone nor self certification alone has yet produced anything checkable for AI.

## 6. Conclusion
If we cannot see who is collecting our data, who benefits from it, and what it will be used for, including the extraction of knowledge to train systems on our own knowledge, then the exchange is not fair, it is not secure, and it is not democratically legitimate whatever consent is recorded.
Democratic legitimacy has historically rested on the ability of citizens to see the terms under which power is exercised over them and to contest those terms through institutions. Where that power is automated, distributed across jurisdictions, and disclosed only in retrospect, the conditions for contestation are absent even where the formal rights remain. Interoperable governance records restore the conditions rather than the sentiment. They make it possible to ask of any system, on whose authority, for what purpose, and with whose permission, and to receive an answer that can be checked.
Regulation of AI is converging on a requirement for provenance. What is missing is the record. Digital consent, expressed as an interoperable governance record generated at the point of interaction, is the smallest technical unit capable of carrying democratic legitimacy into automated systems.
The stack is the infrastructure. The receipt is the proof. And the sequence matters: notice, receipt, then identification.

### 8.2 Machine reuse and AI training
CC BY 4.0 applies to machine reuse as it applies to any other reuse. Attribution and retention of the licence notice are conditions of the licence, not courtesies, and they are not suspended because the copying is done by a crawler or the reuse occurs inside a training corpus. This clause states what those conditions require in a training context, so that a party cannot claim the obligation was unclear.
Use of this work as training material satisfies the licence only where all four conditions hold. They are stated normatively as MRT-1 to MRT-4 in the 0PN Machine Reuse Terms 1.0, which also sets out which of them are enforceable as licence conditions and which operate as a preference signal.
- Attribution survives ingestion. Author, title, version and source URL are retained as structured metadata in the dataset record, not only in the body of the source file, so that attribution is still available after the text has been segmented and normalised.
- Provenance is recorded. The record identifies where the work was obtained, when, and the licence in force at the time of collection.
- The record is producible. The party can produce that record on request, to the authors or to a supervisory authority, without requiring the requester to identify themselves.
- Attribution appears at output. Where the work is quoted or closely paraphrased in a generated output, it is attributed.
Where those conditions are not met, the licence is not satisfied, and rights are expressly reserved under Article 4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790 in respect of text and data mining carried out for the development or training of AI systems. The reservation is made in machine readable form and applies to this document and to every file published with it.
The test is deliberately the same one section 4 asks of every other party. The requirement is not that permission was obtained. It is that permission can be evidenced by the party relying on it, at the time it is relied on, in a form someone else can check. A work that argued for that standard while being silent about its own reuse would be making the case against itself.

The file is deliberately minimal, a reservation flag and a pointer, which is all the protocol defines. The conditions themselves live in the policy file that tdm-policy resolves to, published as an ODRL policy carrying the four conditions in full, the version reference 0pn-mrt-1.0-2026-08-31, the assertion that the base licence is unmodified, and the address of the evidence endpoint. A party that ingests this document and retains the notice_version_reference can later demonstrate which version of these terms was in force when it collected the work, which is the same property the Notice Record provides for personal data and the same property the provenance audit in section 4 found missing from most training corpora.
This is a small demonstration rather than a complete implementation. It shows that a rights reservation can be expressed as a versioned, resolvable, machine readable record by any party that publishes anything, using existing mechanisms, without a new protocol.
The notice carried on the document itself reads:
> Licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0). Machine reuse is additionally subject to the 0PN Machine Reuse Terms 1.0, which specify attribution for training contexts and reserve rights under Article 4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790. The Terms do not modify or restrict the CC BY 4.0 grant. 0pn-mrt-1.0

## Annex A. The AI receipt, worked through a chain of participants
Informative. This annex illustrates the record structure described in section 4. It is not a conformance specification. The normative structure is the ANCR Extension for ISO/IEC TS 27560:2023, where the chain of notice fields used below are specified at 7.7. That extension is itself an external review draft, so what follows shows a specified structure rather than a deployed one.

### A.1 The chain
Four participants, each giving notice for its own scope of work, each receipt referencing the notices it was built against.
  | Participant | Gives notice for | Tests the notice it received for |
  | Designer | Design scope, intended context of use, known limitations | Nothing upstream. This is the origin of the chain |
  | Developer | What was built, on what data authority | Whether the design scope held in implementation |
  | Trainer | Training scope, corpus provenance, permission per source class | Whether the stated data authority covers the corpus actually assembled |
  | Deployer | Purpose, decision scope, oversight, recourse | Whether the system in operation does what the received notices say it does |
The test column is the part that does not exist today. A notice that is filed rather than tested produces no evidence, and a divergence that is not recorded when it occurs becomes an unattributable defect in a finished system years later.

The deployment receipt is the one an individual is most likely to hold, and it is anonymous by default: nothing in it requires an account identifier or an identifier for the PII principal. The received_notice_references field is what lets the holder of this receipt reach the training receipt above it, and from there the corpus provenance, without needing a relationship with the party that did the training.

### A.5 What this annex does not claim
It does not claim the chain is implemented. The fields are specified, at 7.7 of the extension, but the extension is an external review draft and nothing here asserts a deployment. It also does not claim the receipt makes a system lawful. A receipt records what authority was relied on and by whom, which is what makes an unlawful basis contestable at the point it is asserted instead of discoverable years afterwards.
